The terminal benchmark

Airports present the wait as time to eat, shop, rest and explore. This benchmark checks what is actually measured about terminal time in six regions, and what 28 leading airports offer for it, using only data from the last ten years.

Research questionWhat do people do in airport terminals around the world, what do airports offer for that time, and how much of it is measured?

The report in 60 seconds

Three takeaways · each with its source
  1. 37% Fact

    Most terminal time goes to the gate, and to food

    In a 2023 US panel, 37% of flyers went straight to the gate and waited, 45% went to the gate and then backtracked, and 18% looked around first. Across 89 US airports, longer dwell time raised food and drink revenue per passenger but not retail.

    Source: National Academies [1]; Wu, Morlotti and Mantin [2]
  2. 36% Interpretation

    Shopping is losing its share of the wait

    36% of international travelers entered a duty-free store in the first half of 2026, against about four in ten before Covid. Airport non-aeronautical revenue per passenger fell from US$8.61 in 2017 to US$7.57 in 2024, and Heathrow’s retail revenue per passenger is 21% below 2019 while its food and drink income per passenger rose.

    Source: m1nd-set via TRBusiness [13]; ACI World [14]; Pragma for the UK CAA [15]
  3. 3 of 28 Hypothesis

    Airports are building for an activity nobody counts

    The 28 airports publish what they offer, from gardens and museums to sleep cabins, sensory rooms and free city tours, but only three published a visitor or participant count for a featured amenity: Jewel Changi, Madrid’s play areas and Pudong’s free tour. The author expects use of non-commercial amenities to be small relative to traffic; that is not yet observed.

    Source: Jewel Changi [23]; Aena [24]; Trip.com via Meet in Shanghai [25]

Fact: stated by the cited source. Interpretation: the author’s reading of the cited evidence. Hypothesis: expected from the evidence but not yet observed. Figures inside every takeaway are sourced.

Summary

The short version

The benchmark matrix has 112 cells (16 activity measures by 7 regions). 20 have a measure from the last ten years, 3 are rated strong, and none is in Latin America or Africa. Alongside it, 27 featured services at 20 of the 28 airports were checked on operator or official pages.

The wait is mostly gate time.

37% go straight to the gate; 45% go to the gate first and then backtrack. 72% want to reach the gate in under 30 minutes with carry-on only. Confidence: medium; one US panel and a global stated-preference survey.[1][6]

Food wins the extra minutes.

10% more dwell time goes with 8% more food and drink revenue and 6% more retail revenue, but only food rises per passenger. At Heathrow, food and drink income per passenger rose 56% from 2018 to 2025 while retail fell. Confidence: medium to high; observed revenue, US and UK only.[2][15]

Duty free is structurally lower.

Footfall 36% (about 4 in 10 before Covid), purchase rate 22% (29% in 2016), conversion 63% (74%). Spend per passenger also slipped at Frankfurt and Paris in 2025. Confidence: medium; syndicated research via trade press, and operator results.[13][17][18]

Lounges pull time away from the concourse.

73% have visited a lounge or premium service at least once; 52% of regular travelers use lounges; 39% of US lounge visitors go there to avoid high-priced terminal food. Confidence: medium; mostly vendor-commissioned.[8][10][5]

Services cluster by region; use is rarely counted.

In this scan, therapy-animal programs were found only in North America, and free layover tours and in-terminal transit hotels at Asian, Turkish and Ethiopian hubs. Of the services checked, only three published a visitor or participant count. Confidence: medium on presence; gap on use.

What cuts against the thesis. The evidence does not show terminal time filled with amenities. (1) 37% of US flyers go straight to the gate and stay there.[1] (2) Passengers say they want speed: 72% want to reach the gate within 30 minutes, not counting shopping.[6] (3) Non-aeronautical spend per passenger is falling, not rising.[14] (4) A widely shared 2020 story about airport amenities “sitting unused” was about the pandemic collapse (SFO concession sales down 96% in May 2020), not a lasting trend, so it is not used as evidence here.[26]

Where the time goes

The gate, the food court and the lounge

Passengers spend roughly one to two hours at the airport and say they want less; the extra minutes go to food, while shopping and duty free slip and lounges absorb the premium end.

Arrival and dwell. In the US panel, 35% typically arrive two hours before departure; flyers aged 18 to 54 prefer up to 45 minutes in the terminal and older flyers an hour or more.[1] Location data for 89 US airports put the average of median dwell times at about 80 minutes, from 39 at Cleveland to 113 at Atlanta, before the pandemic.[2] Globally, 72% of passengers want to reach the gate in under 30 minutes with carry-on only, and 76% within 45 minutes with a checked bag, not counting shopping and dining.[6]

Food, not shops. A 10% rise in dwell time goes with 8% more food and drink revenue and 6% more retail revenue, but per passenger “dwell time has a significant influence solely on food and beverage”, and at airports with several concourses it has no effect at all.[2] Median sales per departing passenger in 2024 were $7.82 for food and drink and $4.39 for retail across 79 US and Canadian airports.[3] In Europe, Heathrow’s food and drink income per passenger rose 56% from 2018 to 2025 while retail per passenger fell 21% against 2019; in 2025 alone Heathrow retail per passenger rose 1.7% to £9.36.[15][16] Frankfurt’s retail revenue per passenger fell from €3.35 to €3.29, blamed on weaker shopping, and Paris spend per passenger slipped 1.2% to €31.70.[17][18]

Duty free is visited less. m1nd-set’s tracking finds 36% of international travelers entered duty free in the first half of 2026, against about four in ten before Covid; the purchase rate fell from 29% in 2016 to 22%, and conversion from 74% to 63%.[13] Globally, non-aeronautical revenue per passenger fell from US$8.61 in 2017 to US$7.57 in 2024, about 12% below 2019.[14] In Asia-Pacific and the Middle East, an ACI study finds dwell time has risen since Covid but “is not always translating into commercial engagement”.[19]

The lounge as a second terminal. 73% of travelers say they have visited a lounge or premium service at least once, and 52% of regular travelers use lounges when they travel.[8][10] In the US, 39% of lounge visitors use lounges as respite from high-priced terminal food and 45% to escape terminal crowds; 46% say their lounge is usually moderately or severely crowded.[5]

Satisfaction and spend. North American passengers who rate their airport experience “perfect” spend an average of $47.60 in the terminal, $20.22 more than those who rate it “just OK” (about $27, the author’s arithmetic); perfect journeys are 12% of the total.[4] The direction of cause is not shown.

Why it matters. If extra minutes buy coffee rather than goods, the commercial case for longer dwell rests on food and lounges, and design that moves people quickly to the gate may cost little.[2]

Reading the evidenceMedium to high confidence that extra dwell time goes to food rather than retail in the US and UK; medium that duty-free engagement is lower than before Covid; low for other regions, where the evidence is vendor surveys and single operators.

What airports offer

Built for dwell, rarely counted

Leading airports compete on gardens, culture, rest and care, and the offers cluster by region; almost none say how many people use them.

What was checked. Three research inputs listed services at 28 airports chosen from the world’s busiest and best-rated, with two each added for Latin America and Africa. The services below were then checked on an operator or official page in October 2026; anything that could not be confirmed as operating, such as Rijksmuseum Schiphol and the Istanbul Airport Museum, is left out.

Regional patterns. In this scan, therapy-animal programs were found only at North American airports. Free city tours and free transit hotels sit at transfer hubs in Asia, Istanbul and Addis Ababa, where they double as airline marketing. Paid sleep cabins and in-terminal hotels were verified at six airports outside North America: Bogotá, Istanbul, Madrid, Paris, Addis Ababa and Shanghai. Sensory and quiet rooms are spreading fast: Hong Kong opened its Sensory Corner in April 2026 and Helsinki its quiet room in November 2025.[44][50]

Technology that gives time back

Faster processing is mainstream; what the saved time buys is not shown

Half of passengers have used biometrics and government counts run into the millions, but the claim that saved minutes turn into spending rests on what passengers say.

Adoption. 50% of passengers have used biometrics somewhere in their airport journey, up from 46% in 2024, most often at security (44%); 85% of users are happy with it.[7][6] SITA finds that “nearly 7 in 10” travelers are comfortable with biometric scanning.[12]

Counts. Dubai airports cleared 9,024,736 passengers through Smart Gates and 439,321 through the Red Carpet corridor in the first half of 2026.[21] India’s Digi Yatra has handled over 100 million journeys across 38 airports; the government says entry checks fell from 15 to 5 seconds, a claim not independently measured.[22]

Booking ahead. Schiphol lets departing passengers book a free security time slot up to three days ahead.[31] Heathrow’s free Reserve & Collect lets passengers reserve goods in most shops and pay on collection.[52] Denver ran a reservable security lane as a pilot in 2023; its current status was not confirmed.

Does saved time become spend? 45% of travelers say technology saves them 10–30 minutes and 60% say it makes them spend more at the airport.[8] No observed study linking faster processing to higher spend was found.

Why it matters. If processing keeps getting faster while spend per passenger falls, the freed minutes are going somewhere other than shops: the gate, the lounge or the phone.[14]

Reading the evidenceHigh confidence on adoption counts; medium on survey shares; low on whether time saved turns into spending.

Method, limits and flags

Read before citing

Question. What do people do in airport terminals around the world, what do airports offer for that time, and how much of it is measured? The report builds a matrix of activities by region, fills each cell from the best source found, rates the evidence rather than estimating missing cells, and benchmarks services at 28 airports.

Scope. Global, tagged by region (North America, Latin America, Europe, Middle East, Africa, Asia-Pacific), with a global column for multi-country surveys. Statistics use data collected since October 2016; services had to be operating, confirmed in 2025 or 2026. The 28 airports (full names in the airport key): ATL, DFW, ORD, DEN, LAX, SFO, YVR; MEX, BOG; LHR, IST, CDG, AMS, MAD, MUC, HEL; DXB, DOH, RUH; CPT, ADD; SIN, ICN, HND, HKG, PVG, DEL, KUL, drawn from ACI World’s 2025 busiest airports and the Skytrax 2026 top 20, plus two each for Latin America and Africa.

Three research inputs. Claude, Gemini and ChatGPT answered the same research prompts independently, and their answers were compared item by item. Conflicts, single-source statistics and every figure or service used here were then checked at the original source. Inputs returned: Claude 29 statistics and 71 services; ChatGPT 16 and 140; Gemini 37 and 168. Checks found problems in each: a summary tool led Claude to drop a real figure (caught by ChatGPT); ChatGPT ran without the shared brief, so its categories were mapped by hand; Gemini missed the US panel survey, gave a 2017 figure as 2019, cited four Bogotá services to a page that does not mention them, and listed a garden at O’Hare that the cited page does not show. Rows not used in the report were not all checked and remain leads.

Sources. Government and operator counts first, then peer-reviewed studies, industry-body surveys and research firms, with vendor-commissioned surveys labeled and capped at medium, and trade press used to trace results. Searches ran from 22 to 24 October 2026 and were not a systematic review; “none found” means not found, not absent.

Flags and open questions

  1. US panel survey. A non-probability panel weighted to the census. Its text says 45% go straight to the gate while its figure shows 37% for that answer and 45% for going to the gate and then backtracking; the figure is used, as in the parent report.
  2. Dwell-time study. Uses phone-location data that cannot separate time before and after security, and data from 2017–2019, before the pandemic. Its revenue figures are totals per airport; the per-passenger result is the one quoted for shopping.
  3. Airport-reported dwell. ACI-NA does not define dwell time and the responding airports change between survey years.
  4. Spend as a proxy. No source counts how many passengers eat or shop; spend per passenger and revenue stand in for both, which mixes price and volume.
  5. Vendor surveys. Priority Pass, Airport Dimensions and SITA sell lounge access or airport technology; the ACI traveler survey was supported by IDEMIA, NACO and Plaza Premium Group. All are rated no higher than medium. The Asia-Pacific Priority Pass figures come via trade press.
  6. m1nd-set. The decade figures are reported by trade press; the underlying sample is not published.
  7. Heathrow. The −21% (against 2019) and +1.7% (2025 on 2024) retail figures use different bases and are both correct.
  8. Dubai counts. GDRFA reports for “Dubai airports”, not DXB alone.
  9. Services. Checked on operator or official pages, except SFO, whose site blocked the author’s tools (confirmed by two research inputs and a September 2025 news report), and LAX’s PUP program, whose operator page is undated (confirmed by a May 2025 news report). Pudong’s cabin count is from July 2025.
  10. Gaps. No observed behavior outside the US, no split of dwell time before and after security, no activity data for Latin America or Africa, and no share of passengers for media, work, reading, sleep or wellness anywhere.
  11. Scoring. The strength thresholds are the author’s choice; a different cut-off would move several cells.
