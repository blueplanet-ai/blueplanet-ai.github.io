One cabin, several people

Several people share many car trips, but the car is built around one of them. This brief sizes that problem against issues the industry already treats as priorities, looks at what has been tried, and invites solutions.

Summary

The short version

The car is designed around one person, the driver. Everyone else in it is a guest using their own phone. Measured in hours, the time Americans spend riding as passengers is comparable in scale to the time they lose to congestion (the two measures differ) and about ten times the time they spend flying. Yet unlike congestion, distraction or charging, this brief found no widely used measure of its cost, and decades of attempts have not produced a good answer. This brief does not propose a product. It sets out the evidence and invites solutions.

Framing · author’s hypothesisThe shared cabin may be a coordination problem more than a missing gadget. Phones, voice assistants, seat and audio controls each serve one person or one device. In the sources reviewed, none coordinated the passengers, their phones, the car’s state and the driver’s attention as one experience. This is the author’s interpretation, not a finding; chapter 6 offers illustrations and the questions a solver would need to answer.

Shared trips are common, and passenger time is large.

Household vehicles carried an average of 1.5 people in 2022; vans carried 2.1 and SUVs 1.7.[2] Together they carried about 0.79 trillion passenger-miles that year, roughly 70 to 104 hours a year per person (author’s arithmetic). That is comparable in scale to the 63 hours Americans lost to congestion in 2024, although riding time and delay are different measures.[1][5]

Passenger screens, as built, mostly go unused.

35% of owners with a passenger screen have never used it, only 6% use it on every drive, and passenger screens draw 21.3 problems per 100 vehicles.[8]

Passengers, especially children, are the ones who feel unwell.

University of Michigan engineers estimate that about a third of adults and half of children are affected by motion sickness, and that passengers suffer it far more than drivers. In a UK survey, 34% of drivers with children said their children get car sick.[12][13]

The pieces exist, mostly in China; no whole answer turned up in the sources reviewed.

Seat-zoned voice, rear-seat entertainment and motion-sickness drive modes are already in production. No concept found combines knowing who is asking, what the driver’s attention allows, and how passengers’ own phones fit in (author’s analysis).[24][30]

Large, unmeasured, unsolved.

Congestion, driver distraction, phone integration and EV charging each have a cost figure and an owner. The shared cabin has neither. That gap, more than the technology, is why the problem persists (author’s interpretation).

Chapter 1 of 7
1
Problem

A cabin built for one

The car knows one person, the driver; everyone else is a guest using their own phone, and anything offered to passengers must not cost the driver attention.

Shared trips are common. Household vehicles carried an average of 1.5 people in 2022; vans carried 2.1 and SUVs 1.7.[2] Weighted by distance, household vehicles carried about 1.43 people per vehicle-mile (author’s arithmetic from NHTS: 2.64 trillion person-miles over 1.85 trillion vehicle-miles).[1] FHWA’s own mileage-weighted occupancy factor is 1.52, which would imply about 0.96 trillion passenger-miles; this brief uses the lower figure.[42]

Passengers cannot always use the time. University of Michigan engineers estimate that motion sickness affects about a third of adults and half of children, and that passengers suffer it far more than drivers.[12] This is an attributed estimate, not a measured population figure. In a UK survey of 2,021 drivers, 34% of those with children said their children have been car sick; 27% of those avoid car travel with them altogether, and 41% of drivers with children dread long journeys with them.[13]

Passenger screens, as built, mostly go unused. In J.D. Power’s 2026 Tech Experience Index (68,084 owners), 35% of owners with a passenger screen had never used it and 6% used it on every drive; the screens drew 21.3 problems per 100 vehicles.[8] This shows that passenger screens as built are not earning their place; it does not show whether passengers would use or pay for a better-integrated experience. One truck maker’s engineer reportedly found that front passengers prefer their own phone and more storage, while rear passengers are often children without phones.[36]

Every passenger request competes for the driver’s attention. 3,208 people died in distraction-affected crashes in the US in 2024.[6] That count covers every kind of distraction, not only passenger requests. Of owners who reported a distraction-related problem in J.D. Power’s 2026 quality study, 46% pointed to the infotainment or touchscreen.[9] Crash risk rises when teen drivers carry teen passengers.[7]

Problem statement · author’sSeveral people share the trip, but the cabin serves one of them. Passengers’ time goes unused or turns unpleasant, children get sick or bored, and anything the car offers them must not cost the driver attention.

Chapter 3 of 7
3
Timing

Why now

The hardware is already in the cabin, assistants can hold a conversation, and regulation now draws the line around the driver’s attention.

Seat awareness is becoming software. Analog Devices says its personal sound zone technology runs on speakers already fitted and could be sold as a paid feature after purchase.[18] Hyundai’s Gleo AI applies “turn on my heated seat” to the seat the request came from.[25] Garmin’s Unified Cabin routes audio and video to individual seats.[23]

Conversation is no longer the hard part. Every assistant in this site’s benchmark already understands natural language; the spread is in what it can operate.[40] The missing pieces are knowing who is asking and what the driver can afford at that moment.

Regulation sets the driver’s boundary. The EU’s distraction warning is required in all new cars from July 2026, which makes the rules for passenger features clearer.[38]

Other markets already treat the rear seat as a customer. In China, close to 900,000 new cars with rear entertainment screens were sold in 2024, up 122%, mostly premium SUVs and MPVs.[37]

A way to charge for it exists. A volume carmaker includes in-car gaming in a connected-services tier priced at $149 a year after the free period.[14]

Invitation

Business model and entry wedges

Three routes to revenue exist today; four starting points look open to a solver.

Ways to get paid

Through the carmaker

  • License or per-car software fee, usually through a tier-one supplier.
  • Long design cycles; suppliers can bundle the same feature.
  • An idea, with no example found: content or game platforms paying carmakers or software providers for built-in delivery to several passengers.

As a paid tier or upgrade

  • Connected-services tier or a feature unlocked after purchase.
  • The only public price found is a volume carmaker’s $149 a year tier[14]; take rates are not public.

Aftermarket and retrofit

  • Products for the cars already on the road.
  • Motion Sync’s route[21]; Holoride shows the risk[22].

Why no whole answer yet: the author’s hypotheses

If most of the pieces exist, why has no one joined them? These are possible reasons to investigate, not findings of this brief.

  1. Split ownership. Voice, audio, screens, seats and climate usually come from different suppliers and teams.
  2. Closed vehicle data. Access to seats, microphones and vehicle state differs by carmaker, and a phone cannot reach most of it.
  3. Long design cycles. A feature agreed now reaches cars years later.
  4. Hard to know who is asking. Seat awareness and permission rules are needed, and this brief did not find them settled.
  5. Driver safety limits. Anything shown or said to passengers must not reach the driver’s view or attention.
  6. Unproven payment. Owners often do not use passenger screens, and no source reviewed shows passengers paying for a better experience.
  7. No shared standard found. This brief did not find a common way to link passengers’ phones with cabin systems; it did not search for one in depth.

Illustrations to spark your own ideas

These are examples of the kind of answer a solver might explore. They are the author’s illustrations, not recommendations and not validated: each builds on the evidence above and would need testing with real passengers, drivers and carmakers.

A coordination layer between phones and the cabin

  • Each passenger’s phone acts as the controller, while the car supplies seat position, audio and vehicle state, a rule for what the driver can safely be shown, and a rule for who may ask for what.
  • Pieces exist: phones as game controllers[14] and seat-zoned voice[44]. Open question: who owns the layer, the carmaker, a supplier or a new company?
Author’s illustration

Comfort for passengers in electric cars

  • Motion sickness may matter more in electric cars, where regenerative braking and quick acceleration change how a ride feels. The trade-press piece cited here describes the link, and Xiaomi and Honda tune their drivetrains for it.[30][31]
  • A cabin-level answer could combine drivetrain tuning with seat cues and screens. Open question: how much relief, for whom, and who pays?
Author’s illustration

Passenger features that run in the car, not the cloud

  • Seat-aware requests, per-seat audio and comfort settings handled on the car’s own computer could work without a connection and keep voices and audio off the cloud.
  • Components already run on existing hardware: seat-aware voice on existing microphones[16] and sound zones on existing speakers[18]. Open question: what can the car’s computer carry safely?
Author’s illustration

Questions any solver would need to answer

  1. Which passenger problem comes first? Motion sickness, boredom, fights over the screen or audio, or requests that distract the driver.
  2. Who feels it most? Families on long trips, the daily school run, or groups of adults.
  3. What do passengers do today? Phones, headphones, a tablet, or nothing.
  4. Who is the user, who benefits, who buys and who distributes? The passenger has the problem, but the owner, the carmaker or a supplier may control the purchase.
  5. Who may ask the car for what? A rear passenger changing the temperature is different from one changing the route.
  6. Can it work across cars and stay safe for the driver? Access to seats, audio, microphones and vehicle data differs by carmaker.
  7. Who captures the value? A carmaker’s own team, a supplier or a new company.

Judgment

Risks, potential and complexity

Exposure is large and solid; demand, payment and the supplier squeeze are the open questions.

Demand is unproven. Owners don’t use passenger screens, and stated interest often doesn’t become payment.[8]

Suppliers move fast. Audio, voice and cabin suppliers can add seat awareness as a feature, which squeezes startups; the best-known passenger startup so far, AirConsole, now belongs to a supplier.[15]

Privacy. Reusing driver-monitoring cameras for passenger features faces the EU’s closed-loop rules; voice and audio approaches avoid cameras.[38] How the rules apply to passenger features has not been checked against the official texts for this brief.

Measurement. No study measures the cost of unserved passenger time directly. The exposure figure is solid; the pain is shown through proxies.

Potential vs complexity · author’s viewPotential: high on exposure, unproven on demand and payment. Complexity: medium to high. Passenger hours show exposure, not proof of need. Exposure per person is comparable in scale to congestion delay and about ten times flying in hours. Most hardware already exists; the hard parts are orchestration across phone and car, rules for the driver’s attention, and carmaker integration cycles.

Method, limits and flags

Read before citing

Question. Is the shared car cabin, where a driver and passengers ride together, a problem large enough to be worth solving, and where could a solution start?

Sources. Official statistics first (FHWA, BTS, NHTSA, USDOT), then benchmark studies (J.D. Power, TTI), then company releases and trade press. Each reference carries its type and date.

Arithmetic (author’s). Household-vehicle person-miles = 23.69 daily miles per person × 305.561 million people × 365 = 2.64 trillion; minus 1.848 trillion household vehicle-miles = 0.794 trillion passenger-miles (NHTS 2022).[1] Passenger-hours = passenger-miles ÷ an assumed 25 to 37 mph (the upper speed is what DOE’s reported driving time and distance imply) = 21.5 to 31.8 billion hours, or 70 to 104 hours per person. Illustrative value of time (not a loss, a market size or willingness to pay) = hours × $21.80 per person-hour (USDOT all-purpose rate, 2024 dollars) = $0.47 to $0.69 trillion; at USDOT’s $20.10 personal-travel rate, $0.43 to $0.64 trillion.[4] Flying = 1.14 trillion revenue passenger-miles (12 months to June 2026) ÷ an assumed 400 to 500 mph = 2.3 to 2.9 billion hours, or 7.5 to 9.3 hours per person.[11]

What this brief is not. The problem statement, the comparison reading, the phone table, the framing, the reasons no whole answer exists yet, the entry wedges, the illustrations, the tests and the potential-versus-complexity view are the author’s analysis, not sourced figures.

Flags and open questions

  1. The hours figure depends on an assumed speed. The speed range of 25 to 37 mph is the author’s assumption. DOE reports household vehicles driven 64.6 minutes a day and 39.7 vehicle-miles a day; if both describe the same unit, that implies about 37 mph, which sets the top of the speed range and the bottom of the hours range. The units behind those two figures were not confirmed, and a different speed would move the hours.[3]
  2. Denominators differ. TTI’s 63 hours is reported per traveler; the passenger and flying figures are per resident. National congestion delay in hours was not found in the 2025 release.
  3. Motion sickness prevalence. “A third of adults and half of children” is the University of Michigan’s own estimate in an engineering news release, with no sampling method given, not a peer-reviewed prevalence study. The UK child figures are from an insurer’s survey.
  4. Passengers as a distraction source. NHTSA’s 2024 summary gives no passenger figure; the IIHS evidence covers teen drivers with teen passengers only.
  5. Company claims not independently verified: Xiaomi’s 51% figure, Hyundai’s 2030 target, Nissan’s comfort claims. Li Auto’s usage figures date from 2021.
  6. Not covered: EU problem sizing; take rates for paid connected tiers; how EU closed-loop rules apply to passenger features; Chinese demand data.
  7. One-time brief. This brief will not be refreshed; figures are as of 17 October 2026.
