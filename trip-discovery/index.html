Summary

The short version

Expert forecasts and today’s evidence point the same way for the next five years: travelers will increasingly get trip ideas, comparisons and plans as answers, from Google, chat assistants, travel apps and social video, rather than by clicking through pages of results. The checkout moves much more slowly. The author’s central case for 2031, “Answers up front, brands at checkout”, is that whoever owns the answer screen will not usually be the one who takes the payment, carries the booking and handles the cancellation. Trip discovery leaves the click; the booking stays with brands and travel platforms.

We start from a gap.

Surveys put AI use for trips at 28% to 56% of US travelers, depending on who is asked and how. Travel sites measure less than 1% of their visits or bookings arriving from AI assistants. Most of the gap is answers that never send a click, and AI inside Google counted as search.[1][2][3][9]

Answers replace clicks.

Search engines as travelers’ most-used research tool fell from 51% to 36% in about 18 months in Phocuswright’s tracking. In a randomized field experiment, Google’s AI Mode cut outbound clicks by 18.8 percentage points, yet Google reported its fastest query growth in five years.[10][4][11]

Planning becomes a conversation; social stays the spark.

AI trip planners mostly look for activities, ideas and food. 60% of US travelers say social media inspires their trips. The best models still produce a valid multi-constraint itinerary less than half the time in research benchmarks.[2][12][13]

Agents at the checkout are a forecast, not yet a habit.

IDC forecasts 30% of travel bookings executed by AI agents by 2030. Today 8% of travelers are comfortable booking through an AI platform, and Google and OpenAI both leave the hotel or travel company as merchant of record.[7][8][14][15]

Three regions, three setups.

In the US, Google adds hotel booking to AI Mode. In Europe, competition rules have stripped Google’s travel features and excluded the EEA from its new AI travel tools. In Korea and China, Naver and Trip.com keep AI inside their own apps.[14][16][17][18]

Baseline

Where we start in 2026

Travelers say they use AI for trips far more than travel sites can measure; the gap is mostly how answers work, not a measurement error.

What travelers say. 56% of active US travelers used AI for planning, booking or in-destination help on at least one trip in the past 12 months, up from 33% in the first half of 2025.[1] Narrower questions give lower numbers: 39% of US travelers actively use AI to plan trips (Phocuswright), 49% of air passengers worldwide and 47% in North America have used AI for travel planning (IATA, over 10,000 responses), and 28% of US adults used AI to plan a trip in the past six months, up from 19% in August 2024 (Longwoods, 1,000 adults matched to the Census).[10][19][2]

What travel sites measure. Booking Holdings says referrals from AI chat assistants still bring significantly less than 1% of Booking.com’s room nights, though they “accelerated over the last several weeks” of the third quarter, and in every travel category Bain tracked with Similarweb data, AI referrals stay under 1% of site traffic.[3][9] Across 41 UK travel sites, 0.37% of visits came from AI assistants, 88% of them from ChatGPT.[20]

Why the gap. Most AI travel answers send no click; Google’s AI answers are counted as search; and a traveler who asks ChatGPT and then opens the Booking.com app shows up as a direct visit. Survey wording widens the range: Phocuswright’s 56% counts planning, booking or help at the destination. Half of travelers who use AI in search engines still click through to the source.[1] The site’s earlier analysis of this gap is folded into this chapter.

Where the money sits. OTAs took about one fifth of US travel gross bookings in 2025, 100.3 billion dollars, and Booking.com and Expedia take about two-thirds of their consumer bookings direct, through their own apps and sites.[21][3][6]

Author’s read · confidence: highThe five-year question is not whether travelers will use AI, which most already do, but whether AI moves the click, the booking and the customer relationship, and to whom.

Shift 1 · discovery

Answers replace clicks

AI answers become the first stop for trip research; clicks to travel sites fall faster than bookings, and the answer is mostly Google’s.

What experts forecast. Phocuswright says AI is “on a path to replace traditional search as the primary front door” for travel. Its tracking shows general search engines falling as travelers’ most-used research resource, from 51% in mid-2024 to 36% in late 2025.[10] Generative AI platforms such as ChatGPT reached 33% use for trip research, five times their 2024 level.[22]

What AI answers do to clicks. In a preregistered field experiment with 1,100 US Google users, switching people to AI Mode only cut the share of searches with a click to an outside site by 18.8 percentage points, lowered trust in Google’s information and raised searching on rival engines by 11.2 points. Removing AI features raised clicks by 8.8 points.[4] The study is a preprint and not about travel alone.

But Google grows. In the third quarter of 2026 Google’s search advertising revenue rose 12% to 63.3 billion dollars, query growth was the highest in five years, and AI Mode queries were three times longer than traditional searches, one in five using voice, images or documents. Google is now expanding ads in AI Mode internationally.[11] The growth costs something: search revenue growth slowed from 17% in the second quarter, and Google’s chief business officer said “the strong growth in AI Mode queries had an impact because of the lower monetization we were seeing in AI Mode”, alongside currency, the EU’s end of a customs exemption and a post-World Cup comedown.[23][11] In the experiment above, participants chose AI Mode for only 0.6% of their searches; AI Overviews appeared on 36%.[4]

Who gets cited. In 1,357 citations Gemini gave for Tokyo hotel questions, price-led questions (“cheap hotel in Shinjuku”) mostly cited OTAs, while experience-led questions drew a majority of citations from hotels, guides and other non-OTA sources.[24] Expedia says answer-engine optimization and social are two of its fastest-growing marketing channels.[6] Skift’s 2026 megatrends tell travel brands to optimize for AI-driven discovery, “GEO over SEO”, and Bank of America calls agentic AI the most significant theme for online travel in 2026, a challenge to “traditional search-and-click models”.[25][26]

What cuts against this. Forecasts of search collapse have been early before: in February 2024 Gartner predicted search engine volume would drop 25% by 2026; Google now reports its strongest query growth in five years.[27][11] Much of the shift happens inside Google rather than away from it. AI Mode is still a small share of searches, and forced use lowered satisfaction in the experiment.[4]
Author’s forecast · confidence: medium-highBy 2031 an AI answer is the usual first step of trip research for most US travelers under 50, and most of those answers are Google’s, in Search, Maps and Gemini, rather than a new assistant’s. Clicks from search to travel sites fall well before bookings do, and travel marketing shifts from ranking pages to being cited and bookable inside answers.

Shift 2 · planning

Planning becomes a conversation

Itineraries start as AI drafts that travelers check; travel companies’ own planners gain on standalone startups, and accuracy decides how far people trust them.

What people do with it. US adults who used AI to plan a trip mostly looked for attractions and activities (54%), inspiration for places to visit (44%) and dining (42%).[2] In Japan, 61% of people planning a summer trip in 2026 said they would use generative AI to look up local food, build itineraries or compare transport, and the survey’s sponsor said AI is replacing the guidebook.[28] In Cornell’s survey of 1,029 US travelers, AI ranks fourth among planning tools, behind search engines, review websites and official hotel sites; more than 60% name accuracy as the main barrier.[29]

Accuracy is the limit. In a 2024 benchmark of 1,225 realistic trip requests, GPT-4 produced a plan meeting every constraint 0.6% of the time.[30] A 2026 re-test of GPT-5.2, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Gemini 3 Pro and others found the best model succeeded 43% of the time on that benchmark, 15% or less on a harder one, and no model extracted every constraint exactly on a set of Chinese trip requests.[13] Public incidents, such as AI tools sending tourists in Peru to a canyon that does not exist, keep the risk visible.[31]

Travel companies build their own. Expedia bought the AI planner Layla in July 2026. Its chief executive said its natural-language tools “are not driving conversion right now” but give over 60% more information about what travelers want.[32][6] Trip.com says orders assisted by its TripGenie assistant rose about 400% in a year, with nearly 60% of interactions now about booking.[18] Human advisors are adding AI rather than being replaced by it: Fora, a platform for independent advisors, raised money at a 1 billion dollar valuation in July 2026.[33]

Where AI leads, travel changes. Among 2,669 foreign visitors to Seoul, the 23 who named generative AI as a leading information source spread their spending across more categories and visited the city’s iconic heritage sites less.[34] The group is small, but it is the first sign that AI planning changes where tourists go, not just how they book.

What cuts against this. Benchmarks test full itineraries with many constraints; most travelers ask for ideas, which tolerate errors. Luxury travelers in the Cornell study use AI for research but strongly prefer human advisors to put complex trips together.[29] Standalone planners have a weak record: Google closed its Trips app in 2019 and folded its features into Search and Maps.[35]
Author’s forecast · confidence: mediumBy 2031 most US leisure trips start with an AI draft, a day-by-day plan or a shortlist, that the traveler checks against reviews, maps and prices. The planners that win sit inside Google, the big travel platforms and super-apps, which hold live prices and inventory; independent planners survive as features others buy.

Shift 3 · inspiration

Social video stays the spark

Trips are still first imagined on social video; AI turns saved posts into plans, and trust in friends, creators and reviews decides which ideas survive.

Social leads inspiration. 60% of US travelers say social media inspires their trip ideas, but trust in friends, credible creators and reviews decides which ideas move forward, McKinsey found in a survey of more than 1,000 travelers and diaries from 26 travelers. It describes trip planning as a set of overlapping “orbits” rather than a funnel.[12] In Spain, Italy and the UK, 43% say they have booked a trip or activity after seeing a viral post or video, and 48% of Gen Z name TikTok as their main source of travel inspiration (TUI Musement, a seller of tours and activities).[36]

AI arrives later in the journey. In Skift Research’s diary study, AI accounted for 21% of touchpoints at the inspiration stage and 37% across inspiration and research combined, but 6% at booking. US travelers’ comfort with AI for ideas and research (net score 58) is about twice their comfort with AI for booking (27).[37]

From video to plan. Expedia’s Trip Matching turns an Instagram Reel sent to its account into a bookable itinerary, startups turn a TikTok link into stops on a map, and Google’s AI Mode accepts images as questions.[38][39][11] Amadeus calls the result “travel mixology”: travelers combine assistants, Reddit, YouTube and brands’ own tools rather than switching from one to another.[40] The leading content travelers save, and the reviews they read, become the material AI answers cite.

What cuts against this. The social figures are surveys, two of them paid for by travel companies, and none measures how much booking value social video really creates. Social discovery also concentrates crowds: 39% in the TUI survey say they visited a tourist site mainly to take a photo or video.[36]
Author’s forecast · confidence: mediumThrough 2031 social video remains where most leisure trips are first imagined, especially for travelers under 35. AI does not replace it; it converts saved videos and posts into plans and bookings, which makes creators and reviews more valuable as the evidence AI cites.

Shift 4 · booking

Agents at the checkout

Forecasters expect AI agents to book a large share of travel by 2030; today’s products keep the travel company as seller, and business travel moves first.

What experts forecast. IDC predicts that by 2030, 30% of travel bookings will be executed by AI agents; its public summary gives no method or definition.[7] For all US e-commerce, Morgan Stanley expects agentic shoppers to reach about 10% of online retail by 2030 (up to 20%), Bain expects 15–25% of purchases to be initiated, influenced or completed by agents, and McKinsey expects agents to “de-verticalize” shopping, pulling people away from single-category apps such as Expedia toward general assistants.[41][42][43] The forecasts differ mostly in what they count.

What travelers say. In Accenture’s survey of 5,003 leisure travelers in 12 countries (June 2026), 40% would fully hand comparing travel options to an AI agent but only 10% would hand over payment.[44] 44% of US travelers say they would book directly inside an AI platform and 40% would let an AI assistant handle flight and hotel bookings (Phocuswright).[22] Asked about comfort rather than intention, the numbers fall: 8% are comfortable booking through an AI platform and 68% prefer a trusted travel brand (Expedia and YouGov, US, UK and India).[8]

What products do. Google started hotel booking in AI Mode in the US in August 2026 with ten partners including Booking.com, Expedia, Marriott and Hilton; the hotel or booking platform remains merchant of record and handles customer service.[14] OpenAI’s checkout protocol is designed the same way.[15] Of more than 60 travel tools for Claude and ChatGPT checked in October 2026, five could finish a booking inside the chat, all for business travel, plus one for personal trips; Expedia, Booking.com, Kiwi.com and trivago only search and send the traveler to their own sites.[45]

Where agents already book. In China, Alibaba’s Fliggy moved its assistant from pilot to live bookings inside Alibaba’s Qwen AI app in March 2026, for flights, trains and attraction tickets; it reports AI orders up 800% during the Spring Festival campaign and conversion comparable to established OTAs.[46] There the assistant and the travel platform belong to the same company. In the US, small bookings come first: Booking Holdings says OpenTable’s native integration with OpenAI has become one of its fastest-growing channels, “from a small base”.[3] Travel executives expect more: about three-quarters of the 600 Accenture surveyed believe AI will be executing bookings, payments and disruption handling for customers within three years, and slightly more of them name AI-native platforms (21%) than airlines and hotels (19%) as the likely owner of the traveler relationship.[44]

The record. Booking without leaving the search page was announced as inevitable for a decade, until Tripadvisor closed its Instant Booking in 2026, one industry tracker notes.[47] OpenAI reportedly stepped back from in-chat checkout in March 2026.[48]

What cuts against this. Payment rails for agents now exist: Google says it is combining its Universal Commerce Protocol with an Agent Payments Protocol, and business-travel agents already book in chat.[11][45] Google’s third-quarter examples of agent checkout were all retail; travel was not discussed on the call.[11] If loyalty programs and cancellations work through agents, leisure could follow faster than this report expects.
Author’s forecast · business and simple rebooking: medium · complex leisure trips: lowBy 2031 agents book a meaningful share of business trips and simple, repeat purchases such as a known hotel, a flight change or a restaurant table. For most leisure trips the agent shortlists and hands off; the travel company stays merchant of record, owns the cancellation and keeps the loyalty points.

Shift 5 · during the trip

Decisions on the move

The fastest growth is during the trip: travelers ask where to eat, how to get there and what a sign says, first on the phone, then by voice and glasses.

In-trip use is already common. Among Korean Millennial and Gen Z travelers, help during the trip is the most common use of AI (32%), ahead of transport routes and price comparison.[49] US AI trip planners look for activities and dining more than for flights or hotels.[2] Phocuswright’s headline AI figure counts help at the destination.[1]

New interfaces are built for it. Google’s first Android XR audio glasses, made with Samsung, Gentle Monster and Warby Parker, arrive this fall with turn-by-turn directions based on where you face, reviews of the restaurant you are walking past, live translation and ride ordering.[50] In Korea, Naver’s AI Briefing reaches more than 30 million people a month, and its AI Tab, with 10 million users, moves from search to purchases and reservations.[17] The site’s In-Car AI benchmark tracks the same shift in cars.[51]

What cuts against this. Travel-specific measures of in-trip AI use are rare; IATA’s survey measures planning, where flight search (21%) and cheapest fares (19%) lead.[19] In-trip errors cost more (a closed restaurant, a wrong platform), and roaming, battery and privacy limit always-on assistants abroad.
Author’s forecast · confidence: mediumBy 2031 asking becomes the normal way to make small decisions during a trip: where to eat, what to do next, how to get there, what this says. The phone remains the main device; audio glasses and car assistants take the hands-free moments. This fits the site’s thesis that time and attention on the move are the next contested space.

Shift 6 · who keeps the traveler

Who keeps the traveler

Owning the answer screen and owning the customer are splitting apart; channel shares barely move in forecasts, and the US, Europe and East Asia diverge.

Four roles, not one. A booking involves four roles: the screen where the traveler decides, the inventory behind it, the merchant of record who takes the payment, and the company that handles changes and complaints. AI is moving the first role fastest. Today’s AI booking products leave the other three with hotels, airlines and OTAs.[14][15]

Channel forecasts barely move. Phocuswright projects the OTA share of US travel gross bookings rising from 20% in 2025 to 21% in 2028.[21] In the UK, the supplier-direct share of the online market moves only from 78% to 81% over its six-year forecast, and the OTA share drifts from 20% to 19% by 2029.[52] Booking.com says its direct share stayed in the mid-60s percent over the four quarters to September 2026, with its app in the high 50s, and that Connected Trip transactions, which combine several travel products, grew almost 20%; Expedia says two-thirds of its consumer-brand bookings come direct and its business-to-business bookings grew 21% in the second quarter.[3][6]

What incumbents and skeptics say. Trip.com’s chairman, James Liang: “we see AI as an evolution of the travel user interface rather than simply a new traffic channel,” and agents will become “an increasingly important discovery and inspiration channel.”[18] Altimeter Capital’s Thomas Reiner expects OTAs to decline slowly, “like the Yellow Pages — permanently damaged, never deceased.”[47] Airbnb kept out of ChatGPT’s apps.[53]

Judgment, not forecast

Scenarios to 2031

The author’s central case splits by role: answers up front, brands at checkout. One alternative is close; agents booking most leisure trips is the least supported.

How good are such forecasts? Forecasts that a new interface would take over travel booking have mostly been early: Google’s standalone Trips app closed in 2019, booking inside metasearch faded, and Gartner’s 2024 forecast of a 25% drop in search volume by 2026 has not shown up in Google’s numbers.[35][47][27][11] Forecasts of new habits have done worse than forecasts of infrastructure.[62] This report therefore trusts forecasts of AI use for ideas more than forecasts of AI taking over the checkout.

What would change the picture

Quarterly

AI’s share of OTA bookings and direct share

Booking Holdings and Expedia earnings calls. AI referrals rising to several percent of room nights while direct shares hold supports the central case; direct shares falling as agent bookings rise supports Agents book. In the third quarter, Booking said AI referrals were still significantly below 1% of room nights but accelerating; a first stated figure of 1% or more would be the first crossing.[3][6]

As released

Booking tools in assistants

Whether Booking.com, Expedia and major airlines let assistants book inside the chat, and who is merchant of record. Search-only tools support the central case; in-chat booking supports Agents book.[45][14]

Quarterly

Clicks and ads in Google’s AI answers

Alphabet calls and independent studies. Fewer clicks with stable bookings fit the central case; Google extending checkout from hotels to flights and activities would move it toward Platforms absorb AI.[11][4]

Each wave

Comparable survey trackers

Longwoods and Phocuswright, with stable wording. Steady growth supports the shifts; a plateau supports Pushback.[2][10]

Twice a year

Planning benchmarks

Valid-plan rates on TravelPlanner and its successors. Rates well above half would make delegation credible.[13]

As decided

Regulators

The Commission’s DMA decisions, the FTC’s pricing study and consumer-law cases on agents. Strict liability for agents supports Pushback; clear rules could speed Agents book.[16][60][61]

Method, limits and flags

Read before citing

Question. How will AI assistants and agents, social video and new devices change how people discover, plan and book trips, and decide during them, by 2031, and who will stand between the traveler and the seller?

Scope. Leisure travel first, business travel where it shows what may reach consumers. Inspiration, planning, the booking hand-off and decisions during the trip. US first; Europe and East Asia as distinct setups.

Sources. Research papers, analysts’ and research firms’ forecasts, company disclosures, regulators, and major news and trade publications. Each reference carries its type and date. Observed, survey, company and forecast figures are kept apart and labeled.

How the research was done. Claude researched and verified the 2026 baseline and the forecasts; Gemini (research papers, government, Google material) and ChatGPT (analysts, companies, major publications) answered the same written brief; Claude opened their sources and merged the findings. Framing, the six shifts, the scenarios and conclusions are the author’s.

What this report is not. The “author’s forecast” boxes and the four scenarios are judgments based on the evidence cited. Confidence labels describe how well the evidence supports a statement, not a probability. Signposts give a direction and a source, not a numeric threshold.
