01 · Executive summary

The trends are agreed. Their certainty is not.

Every cross-domain foresight list verified for this report names technology, demographics, geopolitics and inequality, and every list that itemises climate names it too. The trends differ most in how certain they are. Demographics are close to locked in. AI is the fastest and broadest force but the least certain in payoff. The strategic risk lies less in any single trend than in how they collide: AI load hits the grid, ageing, defence and debt squeeze the same budgets, and rivalry runs through chips and minerals. The 2026 closure of the Strait of Hormuz has already shown a fast trigger cascading through slow stresses.

Demographics are the anchor

The world adds about 590 million working-age people by 2040, and India plus Sub-Saharan Africa supply about 84% of them. China loses about 132 million, the EU about 31 million. Everyone of working age in 2040 is already born.

AI is physical, and power-bound

Data-centre electricity more than doubles to about 945 TWh by 2030. Published GDP-impact estimates range from about +1% to +7% over a decade, so build for the capex certainty and hedge the payoff uncertainty.

One chokepoint, three trends

Firm electricity, leading-edge compute and refined minerals sit where AI, geopolitics and electrification collide. An investment there hedges several trends at once.

Plans will be wrong on timing

Forecasters underestimated AI and overestimated hydrogen. Pair robust commitments with options triggered by signposts, and re-score the trends every year.

02 · How the seven were chosen

Convergence first, then four-criteria scoring

The shortlist starts from five verified cross-domain foresight lists, then adds the WEF Global Risks 2026 survey of 1,300+ leaders as a risk-based cross-check. Each candidate is scored 1–5 on impact, certainty, speed and breadth. Health, work, space, quantum and cybersecurity were assessed but fold into the seven rather than standing alone.

What each score measures. Impact is how large the effect is on economies and organisations. Certainty is how confident we can be in its direction and magnitude by 2040. Speed is how fast the change unfolds within the 2026–2040 window. Breadth is how many sectors it touches. Each is scored from 1 (lowest) to 5 (highest). The scores are the author’s analytical judgement from the cited evidence, not sourced figures, and the four criteria were set for this report rather than taken from a validated method in the literature.

10 · Cross-trend interactions

Collisions matter more than any single trend

Lawrence, Homer-Dixon and colleagues describe a polycrisis as crises in several systems that become causally entangled when slow stresses meet fast triggers (Global Sustainability, 2024). The map below shows 25 directed links between the seven trends. Mapping the trends onto that model is the author’s interpretation; the sourced or inferred basis of each link is in the table.

AI-power loop

AI demand pulls electrification forward, grid limits throttle AI, and both nudge emissions. Whoever holds firm power and grid capacity captures the AI value.

Rivalry-resource loop

Geopolitics both blocks the transition (mineral and chip controls) and speeds it up (energy security), while mineral concentration feeds back into rivalry.

Fiscal squeeze

Ageing, defence, climate damage and interest costs converge on budgets heading toward 100% debt-to-GDP, with inequality as the channel into politics.

14 · Strategic implications

Robust commitments, plus options that switch on with the signposts

Robust in all four scenarios

Commit
  • Electrify where cost curves already win; lock in grid connections and long-term power contracts early.
  • Automate for shrinking workforces in East Asia and Europe; retain older workers.
  • Build climate resilience against today's ~1.5°C baseline, not historical averages; expect insurance to drive siting.
  • Post-quantum readiness against the 2030 deprecation and 2035 disallowance of RSA/ECC proposed in NIST’s draft guidance (IR 8547).
  • Growth exposure to India, ASEAN and selected African markets.
  • Trust and provenance by default in content and communications.

Options, triggered by signposts

Hedge
  • Regionalised supply chains: dual sourcing for leading-edge chips and rare-earth magnets.
  • AI-scale bets, staged against measured productivity rather than adoption.
  • A fallback footprint for the fragmented quadrants (B and D).
  • Divergent oil scenarios rather than a single peak date.
  • Magnet-free motors and alternative chemistries (LFP, sodium-ion) as mineral hedges.
  • Annual re-scoring that aggregates several institutional views instead of trusting one.

15 · Methods and caveats

Long-range forecasts deserve humility

Megatrend studies rarely show their working

267 studies, 1982–2022

Naughtin et al., Futures 157, Mar 2024. Trend counts ranged from 1 to 25; seven trends over 15 years is within the norm, but it is a design choice.

Experts underestimated AI progress

FRI, Sep 2025

Average probability assigned in a 2022 tournament to AI benchmark outcomes that did occur by mid-2025, and to an AI winning IMO gold by 2025 (it did in Jul 2025). The same forecasters were over-optimistic on hydrogen costs (forecast $3.50–4.50/kg vs ~$7.50 actual). Forecasting Research Institute.

How this report applies those lessons

Anchor on the most reliable numbers. Demographics lead; individual forecasters did no better than "no change" baselines.

Show ranges from several institutions for AI GDP effects, oil demand and fragmentation costs. Aggregation improved accuracy by about 1 standard deviation.

Treat technology timing as low-confidence in both directions, since forecasters were too slow on AI and too fast on hydrogen.

Keep scores explicitly judgemental, and spot-check figures read through summaries against the primary PDFs before external use.

16 · Verification and limitations

What was checked, and what to keep in mind

Seventeen headline figures were re-tested against the official source pages in two independent passes (1 and 11 October 2026). All seventeen are confirmed; one source attribution was corrected (McKinsey’s adoption figures come from its State of AI survey) and two phrasings were tightened (Swiss Re’s insured share; IEA’s export-control exposure).

Known limitations

  • Data vintage. Figures are as of 1 October 2026. Growth and debt use the IMF’s April 2026 projections; the July 2026 WEO Update lowered 2026 global growth to 3.0% and raised 2027 to 3.4%. The October 2026 WEO will be reflected in the next edition.
  • Judgement, labelled. Trend scores, quadrant names, the 2026 scenario marker and items marked “inference” are the author’s judgement. Figures marked “derived” are arithmetic on sourced numbers.
  • Secondary sources. Some figures come via secondary summaries where the primary page could not be retrieved; each is marked where used. Several anchors are dated (for example Rhodium 2022, Goldman Sachs 2023, NIC 2021).
  • Thin far horizon. Outside demographics, AI energy demand, critical minerals and long-run growth, few sources quantify 2036–2040.
  • Policy statements. Claims about laws, regulations and government policy cite official texts. Details of national AI laws are omitted because no official source was retrieved for them.
  • No probabilities invented. Where no published probability exists (for example a Taiwan conflict), the report shows cost estimates or rankings instead.
